# Sleeper's margin outruns every touchdown model

**No. In every test run for this report, a recreational bettor making three $20 Sleeper Picks slips a weekend cannot expect to profit from regular NFL touchdown props, and the "probability play" (stacking the most likely legs into 3-, 5- and 8-pick slips) costs more than leaner alternatives.** Sleeper's multipliers are the prediction-market price plus a fixed markup: of 107 anytime-touchdown and passing-touchdown props checked against Kalshi on October 17, 2026, **none had a positive-value side**, and the best single leg on the board still lost 6.2 cents per dollar. Parlays multiply that markup, so the current plan carries an **expected season result of −$427 on $1,020 staked, with about a 10% chance of finishing ahead**. Exchanges such as Kalshi are cheaper but not free: on the same 220 picks, Sleeper averaged **−13.1% expected value per dollar against −4.5% on Kalshi**, and the 3/5/8 card would cost an estimated $55–$193 a season as Kalshi combos instead of about $419 on Sleeper. That is a smaller loss, but still a loss. Better modeling does not change the verdict. A Poisson model built on Vegas implied team totals and player usage is the solid core; expected-touchdown (xTD) inputs and logistic regression add gains measured in thousandths; gradient boosting overfits; and **no model was more accurate than Kalshi's pre-kickoff price**. The only clearly positive-value plays found were rare Sleeper promo lines and sign-up bonuses, and a hint that Kalshi underprices multi-touchdown games is a lead, not a finding. The practical move is to treat Sleeper as paid entertainment, switch to two-pick slips of the least-bad legs (expected season loss −$151 instead of −$427), take every promo, enter picks by hand to stay within Sleeper's Terms of Service, and confirm you live in one of the 30 states (plus DC) where paid Player Picks run.

## Sleeper sells the market price plus a 12–13% toll on each touchdown leg

Sleeper Player Picks lets you build entries of **2 to 8 picks**, each a More/Less call on a player's line, and every side of every line carries its own multiplier ([Sleeper Player Picks Rules](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/9047931-sleeper-player-picks-rules)). A "Max" entry pays only if every pick hits. "Flex" entries of three or more picks pay a reduced amount with one miss, or with two misses at five or more picks ([Sleeper Flex Contests](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/9261402-sleeper-player-picks-flex-contests)). As of 2026, every entry also sits in a peer-to-peer pool of up to 5,000 entries. Even so, the rules guarantee a payout of the entry fee times the entry's "VS Score" regardless of the pool, and independent entry-screen measurements show the displayed Max multiplier is simply the product of the leg multipliers, cut to the cent, with small adjustments in either direction in some samples ([Sleeper rules](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/9047931-sleeper-player-picks-rules); [Alphadog multiplier measurements](https://github.com/Rodantmat/Alphadog/blob/7f31da5a4498fa946be7f67069f33e16cea98a8c/nba/NBA_MULTIPLIERS.md)). So the price on each leg decides your results, and the first job was to measure how much Sleeper keeps on each one.

The measure is the **overround**: convert both sides' multipliers to implied probabilities and add them. A fair line sums to 1.00, and anything above that is the house's cut. Two live snapshots of Sleeper's lines taken three weeks apart gave almost identical answers ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)):

| Prop type | Overround, Sep 26 (Week 3) | Overround, Oct 17 (Week 6) | Payback per $1 of fair value, per leg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anytime TD | 1.140 (112 props) | 1.139 (88 props) | $0.877–0.878 |
| Passing TDs | 1.130 (30 props) | 1.130 (24 props) | $0.885 |
| Yardage, receptions, attempts | 1.123 | 1.123 | $0.890 |
| First TD scorer | 1.153 | 1.150 | $0.867 |

**Touchdown props carry the highest margin of Sleeper's major prop types.** An anytime-TD leg pays back about 88 cents per dollar of fair value before any parlay compounding, and first-TD-scorer legs pay back even less. Sleeper also **marks up longshot "More" prices the most**: the overround is 1.152 on legs whose fair probability is under 30%, against 1.126 near 50%. An independent capture of Sleeper's NBA board found the same structure, about 1.78x per side and a 12.4–12.6% overround ([Alphadog](https://github.com/Rodantmat/Alphadog/blob/7f31da5a4498fa946be7f67069f33e16cea98a8c/nba/NBA_COMPASS.md)). OddsShopper's review concluded that "if you want the most +EV play on every single leg, Sleeper probably is not your app" ([OddsShopper](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/comparisons/how-to-play-sleeper-picks)).

Next, we compared same-day prices for 107 matched touchdown props. After removing the house's cut, Sleeper's probabilities track the Kalshi bid/ask midpoint with a **correlation of 0.998** and a mean gap of 1.6 percentage points ([Caldo market test](../research/market_test.py)). A simple straight-line fit reproduces Sleeper's pricing almost exactly. The probability implied by a "More" multiplier equals 1.005 × the market probability + 0.065, and the probability implied by a "Less" multiplier equals 1.060 × (1 − market probability) + 0.034. The fit is off by less than one percentage point on average. In plain terms, **Sleeper adds about 6.5 points of probability to every More pick, and about 3.4 points plus 6% to every Less pick**, so a player the market gives a 30% chance to score is sold to you as if his chance were about 37%. Treating Kalshi's midpoint as the true probability, every side lost money:

| Market's chance the player scores | Expected return per $1, "More" | Expected return per $1, "Less" |
|---|---|---|
| Under 30% | −22% | −10% |
| 30–40% | −15% | −10% |
| 40–50% | −13% | −10% |
| 50–60% | −11% | −12% |
| 60–70% | −11% | −14% |

Across the board, "More" legs averaged **−15.8%** (range −26% to −7.6%) and "Less" legs averaged **−10.6%** (range −21.5% to −6.2%). **Not one of the 107 props had a positive side**, and the best leg anywhere was Terry McLaurin "Less" 0.5 TD at −6.2% ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)). The pattern matters for choosing legs: the cheapest places to stand are the Less side of unlikely scorers and the More side of strong favorites, and the most expensive is More on longshots.

Sleeper's public does the opposite. Sleeper's own pick counts show **84–98% of entries taking "More" on anytime-TD props in every probability bucket**; Kenneth Walker drew 11,809 picks, 99.5% of them More ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)). The crowd concentrates on the side Sleeper marks up most. Sleeper's per-leg toll is not extreme by sportsbook standards: an older single-game snapshot measured sportsbooks' one-way "Yes" anytime-TD boards at roughly 27–47% board-level hold ([The Lock Talk, citing Action Network](https://thelocktalk.substack.com/p/youre-backing-the-wrong-running-back)). Next to an exchange, though, it is expensive, because a Kalshi touchdown contract above 35% probability costs roughly 3–5% all-in (detailed below). On top of that, parlays multiply the toll.

## Every added leg multiplies the toll: the 3/5/8 plan expects −$427 a season

A slip pays the product of its legs' multipliers, so each leg's discount compounds. Sleeper's App Store listing says Player Picks can "win up to 1000x real cash" ([Apple App Store: Sleeper](https://apps.apple.com/app/id987367543)). The 8-pick slip is where that headline lives, and it is also where the math is worst. We priced the Week 6 board at Kalshi's midpoints to get the expected result of a $20 slip; each cell shows the chance the slip wins, its payout multiple, and its expected return per dollar ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)):

| Legs | Probability play (most likely "More" legs) | Safest legs, either side | Least-bad-value legs | Random legs (average) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | wins 49.0%, pays 1.69x, −17.2% | 61.6%, 1.33x, −17.8% | 49.3%, 1.76x, −13.4% | −24.6% |
| 3 | 33.3%, 2.21x, −26.2% | 48.4%, 1.53x, −25.8% | 26.4%, 3.02x, −20.3% | −34.5% |
| 5 | 14.3%, 4.06x, −41.9% | 29.1%, 2.10x, −39.0% | 8.5%, 7.89x, −32.5% | −50.7% |
| 8 | 3.6%, 11.72x, −57.5% | 13.5%, 3.36x, −54.5% | 2.6%, 20.11x, −48.0% | −67.7% |

The probability play does what it promises: it wins more often. Winning more often is not the same as making money, because **Sleeper cuts the multiplier in step with the likelihood**. A three-pick of the most likely Mores wins a third of the time but pays only 2.21x. An eight-pick wins 3.6% of the time at 11.72x, for an expected loss of 57.5% of the stake. Even picking the safest side of every prop, which lifts a two-pick's win rate to 61.6%, still loses 17.8% per slip. FantasyLabs documented the extreme case: a seven-pick Max entry of heavy favorites hit every leg and paid 1.80x before a boost ([FantasyLabs](https://www.fantasylabs.com/articles/sleeper-fantasy/)).

To turn per-slip numbers into a season, we simulated 20,000 seventeen-week seasons with the Week 6 board as the weekly menu ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)):

| Weekly plan ($60/week, $1,020/season) | Expected season result | Median | Bad case (5th percentile) | Good case (95th percentile) | Chance of finishing ahead |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current plan: probability play, 3/5/8 legs | −$427 | −$466 | −$843 | +$128 | 10% |
| Probability play 3/5/8, no repeated players | −$439 | −$525 | −$799 | +$136 | 10% |
| Least-bad legs, 3/5/8 | −$364 | −$391 | −$778 | +$164 | 12% |
| Three 2-pick slips, least-bad legs | −$151 | −$153 | −$404 | +$110 | 17% |

The current plan has an expected season of −$427, a median of −$466, and roughly a one-in-ten chance of finishing in profit. Whether the three slips reuse the same favorites or use different players barely matters (−$427 vs −$439). The biggest lever is slip length. **Three two-pick slips of the least-bad legs cut the expected loss to −$151, shrink the bad-case season from −$843 to −$404, and raise the chance of a winning season to 17%.**

Real results show how this plays out week to week. The probability-play card for Week 3 lost all three slips even though 5 of its 8 legs hit. Its three-pick failed because two quarterbacks, Winston and Willis, threw no touchdowns, even while Gibbs scored three. Meanwhile a model-built card of five "no TD" picks (Andrews, Montgomery, Hubbard, Barkley, Kyren Williams) hit every leg at about 15.15x ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)). Replaying the probability play over 2026 Weeks 2–5 is even more telling. The 64 legs hit 80% of the time against a model expectation of 59%, an unusually lucky run. The three-pick won 3 of 4 weeks, the five-pick 1 of 4, and the eight-pick none, and the slips came out about **+$10 on $240 staked when the market-expected result was −$96**. In Week 5 alone, 12 of 16 legs hit and all three slips lost. A profitable month is fully consistent with a losing strategy.

Outside data show the same economics across the industry. Massachusetts regulator filings for August 2025 put pick'em operators' share of entry fees at **21.5% for PrizePicks and 25.0% for Underdog** ([Massachusetts Gaming Commission](https://massgaming.com/wp-content/uploads/MGC-Revenue-Report-August-2025.pdf)), and Arizona's July 2026 figure for PrizePicks was 22.5% ([BetArizona](https://www.betarizona.com/news/arizona-july-2026-sports-wagering-tops-599-5-million)). PrizePicks' legally required New York disclosure shows 70–76% of contestants were net losers over the latest month and week ([PrizePicks NY Prize Data](https://www.prizepicks.com/new-york-prize-data)). In a field experiment, high-volume sports bettors predicted they would break even but lost 7.5 cents per dollar ([Brown et al.](https://mattbrownecon.github.io/assets/papers/jmp/sportsbetting.pdf)), and an analysis of 547 million NFL wagers linked heavier parlay use to larger losses ([Borghesi et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612326007464)). No regulator filing reports Sleeper's own hold, which leaves the first-party snapshots above as the only direct measurement.

## On the same 220 picks, Kalshi costs 4.5 cents per dollar and Sleeper 13.1

The direct way to compare Sleeper with prediction markets like Kalshi is to price identical picks at each venue at the same moment. A live pull on the evening of October 17, 2026 (Week 6 slate) did exactly that ([Caldo venue comparison](../research/venue_compare.py)). Counting both sides, Sleeper offered 244 anytime-TD and passing-TD picks. Kalshi listed 234 of them, every one with a bid/ask spread of 4¢ or less. Polymarket listed 220, but only 6 of those had a spread that tight. Fair probability was taken as the Kalshi midpoint, averaged with Polymarket's midpoint where its book was tight. Exchange payouts assume buying immediately at the ask and paying each venue's taker fee: 0.07 × P × (1 − P) per contract on Kalshi, rounded up per order, and 0.05 × P × (1 − P) on Polymarket's sports markets ([Polymarket fee docs](https://docs.polymarket.com/polymarket-learn/trading/fees)). Here are the 220 picks priced at all three venues ([Caldo venue notes](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/prediction_market_comparison.md)):

| Venue | Average payout per $1 if the pick wins | Average expected value per $1 | Venue had the best price on | Picks with positive expected value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sleeper | 1.947 | −13.1% | 0% of picks | 0 |
| Kalshi (buying at the ask) | 2.167 | −4.5% | 66% of picks | 0 |
| Polymarket (buying at the ask) | 1.906 | −19.2% | 34% of picks | 19, all ~$1 orders (see below) |

**Sleeper never had the best price on any of the 220 picks**, and Kalshi's advantage held at every probability level:

| Fair probability | Sleeper EV per $1 | Kalshi EV per $1 |
|---|---|---|
| 20–35% | −19.9% | −6.5% |
| 35–50% | −12.9% | −5.1% |
| 50–65% | −10.2% | −3.7% |
| 65%+ | −9.6% | −2.6% |

Polymarket is a split case. On "More" picks it was the cheapest of the three on average (−4.2% vs −5.4% on Kalshi and −15.5% on Sleeper), helped by real depth on popular players. Woody Marks, for example, showed 8,137 shares bid at 40¢ and 6,139 offered at 43¢, and Derrick Henry showed 4,100 bid at 57¢ and 4,000 offered at 60¢. On "Less" picks it was the worst (−34.3%, against −3.6% on Kalshi and −10.6% on Sleeper), because buying "No" into a near-empty book means paying almost a dollar against a bid of 1–2¢. Jalen Coker's market, for instance, sat at 2¢ bid and 26¢ ask. The 19 Polymarket picks that looked positive were all 5-share orders worth about $1, resting 1–2¢ below Kalshi; the next level of each book was at or above Kalshi's price. **At a $20 stake, nothing was positive at any venue.**

That answers the "free market" question directly: exchanges are cheaper, not free. There is no house setting both sides, but you still pay a spread plus a fee. Kalshi's cost to buy a touchdown contract, relative to its fair midpoint, breaks down as follows on 354 Week 6 markets ([Caldo venue notes](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/prediction_market_comparison.md)):

| Fair probability | Spread cost | Fee | Total cost vs fair price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 20% | 7.3% | 6.2% | 12.7% |
| 20–35% | 2.1% | 5.1% | 6.8% |
| 35–50% | 1.3% | 4.1% | 5.1% |
| 50–65% | 0.9% | 2.9% | 3.75% |
| Above 65% | 1.7% | 1.4% | 3.1% |

These figures replace an earlier version of the calculation that rounded the fee up on every contract instead of every order, and so overstated costs on cheap contracts (18.5% rather than 12.7% under 20%). Posting a limit order at the bid avoids both the spread and, on most Kalshi sports series, the fee. The catch is that it only fills if someone sells to you, which typically happens when the price is moving against you. Even "no-vig" branding has limits: Novig's fee materials warn that "'No vig' should not be read as 'every trade is free'" ([PredictMarkets Novig review](https://predictmarkets.com/novig/)).

Because the current plan is built on parlays, the more relevant comparison is Sleeper slips against Kalshi Combos (Kalshi's parlay product) built from the same legs. This week's probability-play legs were McCaffrey, J. Taylor and Bijan Robinson to score; Cam Ward, Tanner McKee and Jalon Daniels to throw 1+ TD; Kyren Williams to score; and Jordan Love to throw 2+ TDs. Combo prices come from requests for quotes and could not be observed directly. They were therefore modeled as the product of the legs' fair probabilities, times a markup of 0% or 3% per leg, plus the taker fee. The 3% figure is in line with the per-leg inflation measured on Kalshi combos in other sports ([Moshrefi 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1)).

| Legs | Win chance | Sleeper payout | Sleeper EV per $20 | Kalshi combo payout (0% / 3% markup) | Kalshi combo EV per $20 (0% / 3%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 49.0% | 1.69x | −$3.44 | 1.97x / 1.86x | −$0.69 / −$1.76 |
| 3 | 34.1% | 2.18x | −$5.15 | 2.81x / 2.57x | −$0.88 / −$2.47 |
| 5 | 15.4% | 3.88x | −$8.05 | 6.13x / 5.30x | −$1.12 / −$3.69 |
| 8 | 4.1% | 10.50x | −$11.47 | 23.05x / 18.21x | −$1.26 / −$5.20 |

The gap widens with every leg, because Sleeper's markup compounds: the eight legs that pay 10.50x on Sleeper would pay roughly 18–23x as a Kalshi combo. Over a season, **the user's 3/5/8 card costs about −$24.7 a week on Sleeper (≈ −$419 over 17 weeks), against −$3.3 to −$11.4 a week as Kalshi combos (≈ −$55 to −$193 a season)**. The Sleeper figure comes from a snapshot taken a few hours after the −$427 simulation, with slightly different legs, and the two agree closely. The bottom line is cheaper, not profitable: without an edge over the market, every venue loses at the rate of its margin.

Four caveats limit how far this comparison carries. First, Polymarket's prices are mostly not executable at $20, since only 6 of 220 books were tight and its apparent bargains were roughly $1 orders. Second, Kalshi's combo markups are unobserved. If no market maker answers a combo request it simply does not fill, combos carry the taker fee by default, and a leg whose player does not play settles at its "last fair price" instead of being voided as on Sleeper ([Kalshi Combos help](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/13823820-combos); [Kalshi API changelog](https://docs.kalshi.com/changelog)). Third, state law restricts both exchanges. Kalshi's sports contracts were dark or contested in several states as of mid-October 2026 (see the legal section), and guides disagree on Polymarket US: some list every state except Nevada, while others list several more restrictions ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/betting/prediction-sites); [Saturday Down South](https://www.saturdaydownsouth.com/prediction-markets/polymarket-promo-code/legal-states/)). Fourth, Novig and ProphetX could not be measured. Their fee schedules look attractive on paper: ProphetX charges 0% on parlays, and Novig charges nothing on pre-game straight trades while building a 0.10 × P × (1 − P) fee into parlay quotes ([OddsShopper: ProphetX vs Novig](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/prophetx-vs-novig)). But neither exposed a public price feed reachable for this test (Novig returned 404, ProphetX 502). Whether either lists anytime-TD contracts is unconfirmed, and Connecticut ordered both out in September 2026.

## Vegas totals and usage drive every good model, and Kalshi already prices them in

We compared five families of models walk-forward, meaning each prediction used only games played before that week ([Caldo model backtest](../research/compare_models.py)). The first is a position base rate that gives every player at a position the same chance. The second is a set of naive player rates built from either actual touchdowns or expected touchdowns (xTD), which credit players for the quality of their opportunities, such as goal-line carries and end-zone targets, rather than for whether they scored. The third is the production Poisson model, which starts from the Vegas implied team total and splits the team's expected touchdowns by each player's usage share. The fourth is logistic regression on 22 features, and the fifth is gradient boosting, a more flexible machine-learning method. Accuracy is scored with the **Brier score**, the average squared gap between forecast and outcome: lower is better, and a forecaster who gives every running back, receiver and tight end the same ~20% scores about 0.20. The **calibration slope** checks whether forecasts mean what they say: 1.0 is honest, and well below 1.0 means overconfident. The population was running backs, receivers and tight ends who played, forecast for anytime TD:

| Model | What it uses | Brier 2024 (n=2,617) | 2025 (n=2,587) | 2026 Wks 2–6 (n=714) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Position base rate | Same chance for every player at a position | 0.2010 | 0.1987 | 0.1937 |
| Naive actual-TD rate | Player's own past TD rate | 0.1969 | 0.1986 | 0.1933 |
| Naive xTD rate | Player's past expected-TD rate | 0.1946 | 0.1919 | 0.1820 |
| Production Poisson | Vegas implied total + usage + actual-TD share | 0.1898 | 0.1880 | 0.1820 |
| Poisson, xTD shares | Same, with expected-TD shares | 0.1905 | 0.1874 | 0.1808 |
| Poisson, 50/50 shares | Half actual, half expected | 0.1899 | 0.1875 | 0.1812 |
| Logistic regression | 22 features | 0.1908 | 0.1888 | 0.1805 |
| Gradient boosting | Flexible machine learning | 0.1984 | 0.1941 | 0.1827 |
| Logistic + boosting ensemble | Average of the two | 0.1926 | 0.1903 | 0.1807 |

Two results set the foundation. First, **a player's own recent touchdown rate is nearly useless and badly overconfident**: its calibration slope is only 0.11–0.15, and it barely beats the base rate. Expected-TD rates do better but are still overconfident (slope about 0.7). Outside research agrees that touchdown counts revert while the role that produces them persists. Once a player's baseline is controlled for, trailing receiving TDs actually predict *lower* future scoring (partial correlation −0.128 for receivers and −0.184 for tight ends) ([GWTTKB](https://www.gwttkb.com/research/what-actually-predicts-fantasy-football.html)). PFF found xTD stickier from year to year than actual TDs (0.60 vs 0.52) ([PFF](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-football-metrics-that-matter-touchdown-efficiency)). Second, **the Vegas-plus-usage Poisson model is the reliable workhorse**: it was best or near-best in all three seasons, with a calibration slope near 0.98.

Two results show the limits of refinement. Swapping in **expected-TD shares helps, but only by about 0.001 Brier**. The 2026 gain for the xTD version (−0.0012, 95% interval −0.0026 to −0.0004) and for the 50/50 actual-plus-expected version (−0.0008, interval −0.0015 to −0.0004) are statistically real and practically tiny. And **more flexible machine learning does not pay off at this sample size**. Logistic regression was best in 2026 (0.0016 better than production) but worse in 2024 and 2025, while gradient boosting overfit and finished as the worst learned model in all three seasons.

The ceiling is low. The best models rank players with an AUC of about 0.65–0.66, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect. Each week's **top-10 anytime-TD picks hit 57.3% (production), 58.7% (50/50 version), 57.0% (logistic), 53.0% (gradient boosting) and 51.1% (naive TD rate)** across 370 picks in 2024–26. That is roughly the probability those players' prices already imply. QB 2+ passing touchdowns are closer to a coin flip. A constant base-rate forecast scores 0.2501, and the best models improve only to 0.2368 (logistic), 0.2375 (xTD Poisson) and 0.2378 (production), with gradient boosting again trailing at 0.2445 and AUC at 0.62–0.63. OddsShopper's count of 1,021 starts in 2024–25 found that the over on 1.5 passing TDs hit 45.9% of the time ([OddsShopper](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/betting-101/nfl-passing-touchdown-props)).

The decisive test put the models against the market. Hourly Kalshi price histories for every settled 2026 NFL anytime-TD and passing-TD event (160 events, 4,256 markets) supplied each contract's last tight quote before kickoff. Kalshi's settlements matched nflverse box scores on every matched player-game ([Caldo Kalshi history](../research/kalshi_history.py)). On the 699 anytime-TD player-games with both a model forecast and a tight quote ([Caldo market test](../research/market_test.py)):

| Predictor | Brier | Log loss | Calibration slope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position base rate | 0.1953 | 0.5791 | 0.74 |
| Production model | 0.1835 | 0.5512 | 0.96 |
| Model, 50/50 actual + expected TD | 0.1827 | 0.5489 | 1.04 |
| Logistic regression | 0.1819 | 0.5481 | 0.94 |
| **Kalshi pre-kickoff price** | **0.1813** | **0.5465** | 0.86 |
| 25% model + 75% Kalshi | 0.1810 | 0.5452 | 0.93 |
| 50/50 model + Kalshi | 0.1811 | 0.5451 | 0.99 |

**Kalshi's price was at least as accurate as every model.** Its edge over the production model (0.0014 Brier) is not statistically significant over six weeks (95% interval −0.006 to +0.004), but the direction is consistent. For QB 2+ passing TDs (119 games), market and model were effectively tied (0.2166 vs 0.2170), and blending the two improves accuracy only in the fourth decimal place. Outside work reaches the same verdict. The most complete public anytime-TD backtest (2025 season, about 6,000 priced player-games) found the sportsbooks' de-vigged closing consensus more accurate than its well-calibrated model in both halves of the season (Brier 0.1397 vs 0.1440, then 0.1266 vs 0.1299), and its author concluded that "mixing the model in doesn't help" ([popcawn/nfl-td-predictor](https://github.com/popcawn/nfl-td-predictor)). This is the core problem on Sleeper: **a model that merely matches the market still buys every leg at the market price plus Sleeper's 6.5-point markup on Mores**. To profit, a model would have to beat Kalshi by more than that margin on the legs it picks, and the best public-data model does not beat Kalshi at all.

## Of a dozen strategies, only promotions clear the margin

The strategies recreational and sharp pick'em players use fall into a few families. The table summarizes what the evidence says about each, using first-party tests where they exist and outside evidence where they don't.

| Strategy | What the evidence shows | Verdict |
|---|---|---|

The model-driven strategies deserve a closer look, because they *feel* like edges. We replayed the 2026 anytime-TD legs that had Kalshi quotes, with Sleeper multipliers rebuilt from the fitted pricing rule and a flat $1 per leg ([Caldo market test](../research/market_test.py)):

| Strategy (flat $1 per leg) | Bets | Hit rate | Avg multiplier | Realized return (± std. error) | Market-expected return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bet every side | 1,398 | 50.0% | 2.41 | −14.9% (±3.0%) | −16.4% |
| Model "value" (model p × multiplier > 1) | 157 | 32.5% | 3.73 | −10.6% (±12.6%) | −24.3% |
| Model value, edge > 5% | 98 | 26.5% | 4.26 | −15.0% (±17.1%) | −27.6% |
| Logistic "value" | 148 | 38.5% | 3.59 | +1.1% (±13.1%) | −23.6% |
| 50/50-version "value" | 18 | 27.8% | 4.11 | −52% (±19%) | −26.7% |
| Top-5 most likely "More" legs per week | 25 | 68.0% | 1.63 | −1.2% (±14.7%) | −11.0% |

Read the last two columns together. "Market-expected" is what Kalshi's prices said each strategy should return, and "realized" is what actually happened, with its standard error. **No strategy's realized return can be told apart from the market's expectation.** The model's "value" flags are the trap. They cluster on longshot "More" legs, the same legs the market rates as the worst bets on the board (−24% to −28% expected), which is exactly where Sleeper's markup is heaviest. The author of the public popcawn backtest traced the same behavior in his model to spreading touchdown probability "too flatly". His model claimed about +45% expected value on 1,019 sportsbook bets and realized +3.4%, with an interval of −21% to +30% ([popcawn/nfl-td-predictor](https://github.com/popcawn/nfl-td-predictor)). Logistic "value" landing at +1.1% (±13.1%) against a −23.6% expectation is luck over 148 bets, not an edge, and the top-five-favorites strategy tells the same story. Realized returns across all 2026 1+ TD markets confirm the bucket pattern. "More" on players under 20% lost 42%, and on players at 20–30% it lost 27%, while "Less" on the same players lost only 8.6% and 7.2% ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)).

Three structural ideas are untested rather than disproven. **Correlated stacks** are the first. Sleeper allows same-game and same-team stacks, such as a quarterback's passing-TD More with his receiver's anytime-TD More, as long as the entry spans two teams. One NBA sample showed no same-game discount, whereas Underdog and PrizePicks explicitly reprice correlated picks ([Sleeper rules](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/9047931-sleeper-player-picks-rules); [Alphadog](https://github.com/Rodantmat/Alphadog/blob/7f31da5a4498fa946be7f67069f33e16cea98a8c/nba/NBA_MULTIPLIERS.md); [Underdog](https://help.underdogsports.com/en/articles/11010091-correlated-picks-how-stacking-players-affects-your-payout-multiplier); [PrizePicks](https://www.prizepicks.com/help-center/payouts)). No source has measured whether Sleeper's touchdown lines misprice that correlation, so it remains a hypothesis rather than a strategy. **Flex entries** are the second. Independent measurements indicate that a 3- or 4-pick Flex pays the average of its smaller sub-parlays, which makes it a cushion against variance with about the same expected value, not a way to cut cost ([Alphadog](https://github.com/Rodantmat/Alphadog/blob/2a47f62f88b437d4b6373f90cb9ebf569ebf5c75/nba/NBA_FULL_SYSTEM_CERTIFICATION_2026-10.md)). **Stale-line sniping** is the third, and it is blocked three ways: none of the 107 snapshot props was mispriced, Sleeper's Terms let it void picks "materially inconsistent with prevailing market standards", and monitoring lines automatically violates its anti-scraping rules ([General Terms of Use](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5486620-general-terms-of-use)). One structural feature does favor the bettor slightly: under NFL Injury Insurance, a losing "More" pick is voided when an offensive player leaves in the first half and does not return ([NFL Injury Insurance](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/9869560-nfl-injury-insurance)).

## Promo lines and multi-touchdown pricing are leads, not proven edges

The only clearly positive-value picks seen on Sleeper were promotions. A pull of Sleeper's promo feed on October 17, 2026 found 8 promo lines across all sports, 2 of them NFL ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)). One was a slashed line: an NFL quarterback's passing yards More 0.5 at 1.78x, when his regular line was 227.5 at 1.78/1.78. A starting quarterback clears half a yard roughly 98% or more of the time, so that leg alone is worth about **+74% per dollar**. Paired with the least-bad regular leg in a two-pick, the entry still carries about **+60% expected value**. The other was a multiplier boost on receptions More 6.5, from 2.03x to 2.20x. With a fair probability of about 43.8%, that boost is worth about −3.7%, a smaller loss but not a profit. Promo lines are scarce: an independent probe counted 8 promo lines against 9,120 regular ones ([Alphadog](https://github.com/Rodantmat/Alphadog/blob/2a47f62f88b437d4b6373f90cb9ebf569ebf5c75/nba/NBA_FULL_SYSTEM_CERTIFICATION_2026-10.md)). They also carry entry and usage limits, and Sleeper pays boost winnings above the unboosted amount as play-through credit that expires in 30 days ([Payout Boosts](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/10104136-payout-boosts); [Multiplier Boosts](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/10722683-multiplier-boosts)). Sleeper's obvious-error clause explicitly excludes "clearly designated promotional offers or enhanced multipliers", so a legitimate promo will be honored ([General Terms of Use](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5486620-general-terms-of-use)).

The welcome offer is the other reliable positive: a 100% match of a first Player Picks deposit up to $100 (code PLAY). The deposit and match must be used to enter contests within 30 days before they can be withdrawn ([Sleeper Introduction](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5554544-introduction); [Deposit Match Terms](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/6516011-deposit-match-promotion-terms-of-use)), and some affiliate codes add $20 free ([FantasyLabs](https://www.fantasylabs.com/articles/sleeper-fantasy/)). The match is worth the bonus minus the expected loss on the entries used to clear it, so clearing it with two-pick least-bad entries (−13.4% expected per slip) keeps most of it. But it is one-time, and even a full $100 is smaller than the expected season loss of the cheapest plan tested (−$151).

The more intriguing lead comes from Kalshi's own calibration ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)). Its 1+ TD markets were well calibrated in 2026 (331 "Yes" settlements expected, 324 observed). But **its 2+ TD markets paid out more often than priced: 63 observed vs 45.9 expected overall, and 43 vs 28.8 in the 5–30% band (z = 2.84)**. Its 3+ passing-TD markets showed 34 observed vs 26.1 expected (z = 1.74). Two other facts point the same way. Across 2024–26, 2+ TD outcomes ran 6.5% above what a Poisson model predicts (z = 1.19), consistent with mild touchdown clustering. And 2026 games have scored 1.5 points above the Vegas total (standard error 1.46), after +1.48 in 2024 and +1.15 in 2025. Several things argue for caution. Six weeks is a small sample. Several probability bands were examined, which raises the odds that one looks unusual by chance. The season's extra scoring could fade. And a working paper on Kalshi finds a favorite–longshot bias that, in general, *overprices* low-probability contracts ([Bürgi, Deng & Whelan](https://www.ucd.ie/economics/t4media/WP2025_19.pdf)). **Treat this as possible, not established.** It is also not a Sleeper angle: Sleeper offers only 0.5 anytime-TD lines (and passing TDs at 0.5, 1.5 and 2.5), so the 2+ TD outcome in question cannot be bought there.

The table separates what the evidence establishes from what it only suggests.

| Finding | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Sleeper keeps ~12–13% per touchdown leg, ~11–12% on yardage props | Established | Two snapshots three weeks apart; matches an independent NBA measurement |

## Thirty states plus DC, and a Terms-of-Service line the tool must not cross

Sleeper's own availability pages (August 3, 2026) list paid Player Picks in **Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, California, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Mexico, North Carolina, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Oregon, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, Wisconsin, Wyoming and Washington DC** (DC on iOS only). They exclude Arizona, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Mississippi, Montana, Nevada, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Washington ([Where Can I Play?](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5556045-where-can-i-play); [Where Can I Play? iOS](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/12454375-where-can-i-play-ios)). Players must be 18, or 19 in Alabama and Nebraska and 21 in Massachusetts and Virginia ([Who Can Play?](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5556033-who-can-play)). State rules narrow the menu further. Vermont limits Player Picks to fantasy-point stats only, which as written excludes touchdown props. Arkansas allows full-game projections only. Massachusetts caps deposits at $1,000 a month and Tennessee at $2,500, and seven states bar college athletes ([State Specific Rules](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/7062560-state-specific-rules-restrictions)). Many affiliate state tables are stale and contradict Sleeper's pages; some still list Kentucky, for instance. The in-app geolocation check and Sleeper's own page are the source of truth, and Sleeper itself makes "no representation that participation in the Contests is lawful in any particular state". Anyone who wins $600 or more in a year must supply a Social Security number for tax reporting ([General Terms of Use](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5486620-general-terms-of-use)).

Kalshi's map is shakier. As of October 15, 2026, its sports contracts were dark in Michigan under a state-court injunction, blocked in Washington and geofenced in Nevada. States could enforce against them in Ohio, Tennessee, Utah, Iowa and Connecticut after recent rulings, and cases were pending in several other states ([OddsShopper: Where is Kalshi legal](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/prediction-markets/where-is-kalshi-legal)). Any exchange step in the plan depends on where the bettor lives, and that can change month to month.

For a bettor using a tool, the Terms of Service matter as much as the law. Sleeper's General Terms of Use (updated October 6, 2026) prohibit extracting "any data or content from the Services through any automated means, bot, script, spider, robot". They also prohibit accumulating prizes "through unauthorized methods such as unauthorized scripts or other automated means" (§11.1, §11.3). Under §5.20, Sleeper can restrict the account, withhold prizes and "claw-back unwarranted gains" ([General Terms of Use](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5486620-general-terms-of-use)). The Contest Terms separately ban "automated or programmed methods of entry" ([Contest Terms & Conditions](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/6325720-contest-terms-conditions)). **The Caldo tool's `--sleeper` option reads Sleeper's public lines endpoints automatically. That falls under the prohibition even though the endpoints need no login. The manual `--lines` option avoids it** ([Caldo analysis](../research_notes/Sleeper%20Picks%20profitability%20deep%20dive/own_quant_analysis.md)). The research snapshots in this report, including the venue comparison, came from that feed, and the comparison script flags the same conflict; the bettor's weekly workflow should not. Sleeper also reserves the right to adjust "entry fee limits and payout multipliers, on an individual account basis ... for any reason" (§5.18). It sorts some pools by player experience, and it resolves disputes through individual arbitration with a 30-day opt-out ([General Terms of Use](https://support.sleeper.com/en/articles/5486620-general-terms-of-use)). No public reports of Sleeper limiting winning players were found, but rival PrizePicks has capped a consistently winning user at $5 per card ([OddsShopper: PrizePicks Limits](https://www.oddsshopper.com/articles/betting-101/prizepicks-limits)).

## A $60 weekend that loses less: two-pick slips, least-bad legs, promos first

The probability play was a reasonable instinct: pick what is most likely to happen. On Sleeper, though, likelihood is already priced into the multiplier. Picking favorites changes how often you win, not what you win on average. With no regular-line edge available, the realistic goal is to buy the same weekend entertainment for less, while watching for the rare plays that are genuinely positive.

**Shorten the slips first; it is the biggest lever.** Replacing the 3/5/8 trio with three two-pick slips improves the simulated season from −$427 to −$151 and the bad-case season from −$843 to −$404, and it raises the chance of finishing ahead from 10% to 17%. If a longer slip is part of the fun, keep it to one and keep it short. On the Week 6 board, a three-pick of least-bad legs gave up 20.3% of its stake in expectation, against 57.5% for an eight-pick of favorites.

**Choose legs by how little they lose, not by how likely they are.** Compare each Sleeper multiplier with the Kalshi midpoint, entering Sleeper's numbers by hand in the tool's `--lines` mode, and pick the least-negative sides. In practice, that means favoring "Less" on players with under roughly a 50% chance to score and taking "More" only on strong favorites. It means never stacking "More" on longshots, which cost −22% in expectation below 30% and lost 27–42% in the replay. And it means skipping first-TD-scorer props, which carry Sleeper's highest margin. This is the opposite of what 84–98% of Sleeper's crowd does.

**Check promos before anything else each weekend.** Take every slashed-line promo you qualify for and pair it with the least-bad regular leg. Use the deposit match and clear it with two-pick least-bad entries. Treat payout and multiplier boosts skeptically: a boost on an unlikely leg can still lose, as the −3.7% receptions boost showed.

**Keep the model, but make it a referee, not a value finder.** The production Poisson model, or its 50/50 actual-plus-expected-TD version (the one upgrade with a statistically supported gain), is well calibrated. That makes it useful for judging which legs are least overpriced and for sanity-checking hunches. Do not chase its "value" flags on longshot overs, and do not invest effort in gradient boosting. Log every slip with its market-expected value next to the result: in Weeks 2–5, a −$96 expectation turned into +$10, and without that comparison a lucky month looks like a working system.

**If your state allows it, an exchange is the cheaper way to get the same action.** On identical picks Kalshi cost about 4.5 cents per dollar against Sleeper's 13.1. The same 3/5/8 card as Kalshi combos would cost an estimated $55–$193 a season instead of about $419. Resting limit orders avoid the fee on most Kalshi sports markets but fill less reliably. This lowers the cost of entertainment; it does not create profit, and combo prices remain unverified until you see a live quote. Polymarket is worth a look only for popular players' "More" picks, where its books are deep. Finally, put the multi-touchdown lead on a watch list: track Kalshi 2+ TD contracts in the 5–30% band on paper for the rest of the season before risking money on them.

## Conclusion

On Sleeper, the price you pay decides results far more than the pick you make. The multiplier is a near-exact straight-line function of Kalshi's price, so choosing legs collapses into finding the side with the smallest markup. The comparison of venues puts numbers on that. Moving the same picks from Sleeper to Kalshi changed expected value by more than eight cents per dollar, while the best modeling upgrade moved accuracy by about a thousandth of a Brier point. The models' real contribution was diagnostic: public-data forecasting has already caught up with the market, so a bettor without private information cannot out-pick a venue that charges a markup on top of it.

The plausible sources of positive value all sit outside Sleeper's regular board: time-limited promos and, possibly, Kalshi's pricing of multi-touchdown games. Both are questions of measurement rather than modeling. A season-long log of how often promo lines appear, a few live Kalshi combo quotes checked against the modeled prices, and an out-of-sample check of the 2+ TD calibration gap would say more about profitability than any new model. Until then, a winning season on the current plan (about one in ten) should be read as luck. The changes that matter are structural: shorter slips, cheaper legs, the cheapest legal venue, and every promo the account qualifies for.
